    The Art of Space Planning: A Comprehensive Guide

    Space planning is the invisible architecture of daily life. Before a single wall is painted or a sofa selected, the arrangement of rooms, corridors, and openings determines how a home functions. Good space planning reduces friction, increases comfort, and allows natural light to do its quiet work.

    At its core, space planning is about understanding human movement. How do you enter? Where do you pause? What paths do you take repeatedly? A well-planned home answers these questions without you ever noticing. Here, we break down the principles that architects and interior designers use to shape residential spaces.

    1. Circulation and the 90cm Rule

    The primary circulation path in any home should be at least 90 centimetres wide — ideally 120cm in main living areas. This allows two people to pass comfortably and makes moving furniture possible without dismantling the room. Secondary paths, such as between a sofa and a wall, can be narrower (75cm), but any less creates a feeling of compression.

    Think of circulation as the bloodstream of a home. When it's blocked, everything feels sluggish. When it flows, the whole space breathes.

    2. Zoning Without Walls

    Open-plan living is popular, but without careful zoning it can feel like a warehouse. Use changes in floor material, ceiling height, or lighting to define areas. A rug under a dining table, a pendant light over a kitchen island, or a subtle level change can signal a shift in function without sacrificing openness.

    "A room should never allow the eye to settle in one place. It should smile at you and create fantasy." — Billy Baldwin

    3. The Triangle Rule in Kitchens

    In kitchen design, the classic work triangle — the distance between sink, stove, and refrigerator — remains a reliable guide. Each leg should be between 1.2 and 2.7 metres, and the total perimeter should not exceed 8 metres. This minimises unnecessary steps and keeps the cook focused.

    4. Bedroom Placement and Privacy

    Bedrooms should be buffered from the noisiest parts of the home. In multi-storey houses, placing bedrooms above living areas is common, but consider the direction of sound travel. A hallway with a door creates an acoustic break. In smaller homes, a wardrobe wall or a bookshelf can act as a sound buffer.

    5. Light as a Planning Tool

    Before deciding where walls go, track the sun. In the northern hemisphere, south-facing rooms receive consistent light throughout the day — ideal for living areas. East-facing rooms are best for bedrooms (morning light) and kitchens. West-facing rooms get hot afternoon sun, which can be pleasant in winter but overwhelming in summer. Use deep eaves, pergolas, or deciduous trees to manage this.

    6. Storage: The Silent Requirement

    For every 10 square metres of living space, aim for at least 1.5 cubic metres of dedicated storage. This can be built-in wardrobes, under-stair drawers, or a pantry. Without adequate storage, clutter accumulates and undermines even the most beautiful design.

    7. Furniture Scale and Negative Space

    A common mistake is choosing furniture that's too large or too small. Measure your room and tape out the footprint of a sofa or bed before buying. Leave at least 45cm between a coffee table and sofa, and 75cm between a dining table and wall. Negative space is not wasted space — it's what makes a room feel generous.

    Space planning is both science and art. It requires patience, observation, and a willingness to prioritise how you actually live over how you imagine you might live. When done well, it disappears — leaving only a sense of ease.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    How do I choose the right lighting for a living room? +

    Layer your lighting: ambient (ceiling or floor lamps), task (reading lights), and accent (art or architectural features). Use warm bulbs (2700–3000K) for relaxation, and dimmers to adjust mood throughout the day. Avoid relying on a single central fixture.

    What's the best way to make a small room feel bigger? +

    Use light colours on walls and ceilings, choose furniture with legs (so floor is visible), hang curtains high and wide, and use a large mirror to reflect light. Avoid heavy patterns and bulky furniture. Vertical storage draws the eye upward.

    Are open-plan kitchens still popular? +

    Yes, but with nuance. Many homeowners now prefer a "broken-plan" layout — semi-open with sliding doors or a island that separates cooking from living. This allows for connection while containing noise and smells.

    How do I make a rental feel like home without major changes? +

    Focus on removable elements: freestanding lamps, rugs, curtains, and wall art hung with damage-free hooks. Swap cabinet hardware (keep originals), add a peel-and-stick backsplash, and layer textiles for warmth. Lighting is the single biggest transformation.
